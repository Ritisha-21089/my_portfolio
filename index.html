About

So, who am I?

I'm a backend engineer in Asset Management Technology at Goldman Sachs, where I build platform services that orchestrate reads and writes across internal systems with very different availability guarantees and refresh cadences. Most of my day is spent on the unglamorous half of distributed systems: idempotent writes, retry and compensation logic, reconciliation jobs, and caching hot reference data so the same lookup doesn't cross five services twice.

Before Goldman I spent a year as a research intern at the Complex Systems Laboratory, IIIT-Delhi, writing my B.Tech thesis on deep learning for named entity recognition. I built a fine-grained annotated corpus from scratch, benchmarked ten transformer models on it, and published a reproducible negative result about synonym augmentation that I'm quietly rather fond of — a clean null is still a finding.

What ties the two together is a preference for systems where correctness is checkable. I like problems with a clear notion of "right": a consensus protocol that either maintains its safety property or doesn't, an annotation schema whose agreement you can actually measure. I'm currently looking for backend or applied-ML engineering roles, in India or abroad.

Outside work I play football and badminton, roller skate badly but enthusiastically, and read more than I have shelf space for.

Research

RecipeNER-FG

A fine-grained NER corpus for recipe ingredients, and a ten-model benchmark over it.

Application of Deep Learning for Named Entity Recognition in Recipes

B.Tech thesis · Complex Systems Laboratory, IIIT-Delhi · 2024–2025

Ingredient phrases look simple and aren't. "2 cups finely chopped roma tomatoes, divided" packs a quantity, a unit, a state, a modifier, a cultivar-level name and a usage note into six words, and coarse NER schemas collapse most of that into a single INGREDIENT span. The corpus uses an 8-tag fine-grained schema that keeps those distinctions.

Phrases were sampled from RecipeDB using spherical k-means over TF-IDF vectors so the annotated set spans the lexical space rather than over-representing common ingredients. Quality was measured, not asserted: a 250-phrase doubly annotated subset reached κ = 0.90, with formal tag definitions and a written adjudication process for boundary cases.

Ten architectures were benchmarked under identical conditions. Beyond the headline number, the more useful contribution is a negative one — WordNet synonym augmentation hurt every single model, consistently, by disrupting the distributional cues that mark entity boundaries. It's documented reproducibly so nobody else has to spend a month rediscovering it.
